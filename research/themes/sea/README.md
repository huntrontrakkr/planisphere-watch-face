# The Sea and the Moon: coasts, harbours, tides and navigator's instruments

Status: done (2026-10-16): sources, image index, twelve monthly scenes, corner shortlist, tide tables

Reference set for the "Sea and the Moon" card theme. As the zodiac and
astronomer themes are used (`proto/woodcuts.py`), the card behind the face
carries **one full-card period woodcut or engraving per month**, digitized to
1-bit at about 200 px wide (`engraving()` / the woodcut reader) and shown
faintly, only where the window and the type leave the card open. So what
matters here is strong, clear line work with little dense hatching, and for
this theme a **coast, harbour or sea view with a clear horizontal waterline**
that could be redrawn to follow the real tide (high water: the line rides up
the quay and the hulls; low water: it drops and bares the shore). Corner pieces
for the Time 2 (about 15-20 px) are navigator's instruments. Public domain /
CC0 only.

## Image index

"Waterline" says whether the picture has a clear **horizontal waterline**
that a tide could move: **yes** = a long level line where water meets quay,
shore or hull, with open (light) water below it; *part* = level water but
broken by hulls or drawn as close hatching; no = no usable line. Proportions
are width : height of the file.

## Shortlist: twelve monthly scenes

What the card needs: about 200 px wide (210 for full bleed, as `ZODIAC` and
`ASTRONOMER` in `proto/woodcuts.py`), 1-bit, faint, so open line work with a
lot of white, and here a **level waterline** that can be moved. The card on the
Time 2 is about 200 x 228, so a landscape plate (Hollar about 1.6 : 1, Olaus
about 1.7 : 1 inside its frame) covers the upper or lower two-thirds and wants
a `dy` placing; Schedel's Tarsus (1.2 : 1) and Medina's ships (0.8 : 1) fill
it.

**Recommended set: Hollar's *Navium variae figurae* (1647).** It is a real
series of twelve plates by one hand, CC0, uniform in size and style, mostly
open sky and calm water, and nearly every plate has a long horizontal
waterline across its lower fifth. Several show ships **sitting on the flats
at low water** (347, 352) or careened on the shore (349), which is exactly the
story a tide-following waterline tells. The weak plates are the title (343:
quay and lettering), the slipway (346) and the careening (349), which are
denser and have less water; swap in woodcuts for those three.

- Schedel scans are grey; autocontrast before thresholding. Wipe the caption

## Period tide tables and tide wheels

How 16th- and 17th-century seamen predicted high water: from the **moon's
age** and a constant for each port. At new and full moon ("full and change")
high water falls at a fixed time for each port, later named the
**establishment of the port** (in Waghenaer's day given as the compass point
the moon stands on at high water, e.g. "a south-west moon makes a full sea";
each of the 32 points is 3/4 of an hour, so SW = 3 h after noon). Each day
after, high water comes about **4/5 of an hour (48 min)** later, which is
exactly the 45 minutes of one compass point, near enough, so the rule is
"one point a day". A watch face can show the same: establishment + 0.8 h x
the moon's age, with the waterline in the scene following it.

The three tables in this folder:

- **Medina 1545, Libro séptimo (`medina1545-tidewheel`).** A wheel of 30
  sectors around a moon face, one per day of the moon from "Conjunción"
  (new moon) through i ... xxix, each sector holding a compass rhumb (written
  in by hand in this copy, as far as the hand can be read, Conjunción "Nordeste", i "Nordeste quarta
  al leste", ii "Lesnordeste" ...: one point per day). The caption: knowing
  the moon's age, find the rhumb in the wheel; when the **sun** comes to that
  rhumb or its opposite it is full sea ("será aquella hora plena mar"),
  counting the sun south at noon and north at midnight, and that the sun
  passes from one rhumb to the next in three quarters of an hour. A
  Northeast establishment means high water at 3 o'clock at full and change.
- **Cortés/Eden 1561, "The Table followeth" (`eden1561-tidetable`).** Two
  rings: the inner one numbers the days of the moon 1-30, the outer one gives
  for each day hours and fifths of an hour (day 1: 4/5; day 2: 1 3/5; day 3:
  2 2/5 ...), i.e. how much later than the sun the moon comes to the
  meridian. The black centre holds the four phases as moon faces; the corners
  read "Full / Lowe" and "Myde daye / Myde nyght". Add the outer-ring figure
  to the port's full-and-change time to get the day's high water.
- **Waghenaer 1588 (`waghenaer1588-tidecompass`).** A 32-point compass whose
  inner ring sets ports in the sectors of the points: "Dover, Before the
  Elue, Before the Hever & the Heyder ..." at S, "Hampton, Sluys, St Malo" at N, "Portland, the
  Nase, Texel Roade" just south of E, "Lundy, St Pol" at W and so on: a **tide compass**, the
  port's establishment as the moon's bearing at high water. The outer half
  circles (in the upper half) give the leagues to sail on each point to raise
  a degree of latitude. This is the most direct period source for
  "establishment of the port" and could seed a tiny port list.

Further period tide sources, not downloaded:

- The 13th-century "Flod at London Brigge" table (British Library, Cotton MS
  Julius D VII, St Albans), the oldest known tide table: high water at London
  Bridge for each day of the moon.
- Guillaume Brouscon's pictorial tide almanacs (Le Conquet, Brittany, from
  1546): tide circles with compass points per port for unlettered pilots.
  Copies at the Huntington Library and the British Library; no PD scan found
  on Commons this pass.
- John Seller, *Practical Navigation* (1680, IA `practicalnavigat00sell`,
  same JCB copy as above) has "tables of the moon's age, of the tides" in the
  back matter, in the same moon's-age-plus-establishment form.
- Leonard Digges, *A Prognostication everlasting* (1556 and later), has a
  tide table; the IA copies are Early English Books scans with a ProQuest
  copyright footer, so not used.
